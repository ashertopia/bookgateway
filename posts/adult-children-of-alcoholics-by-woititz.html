Adult Children of Alcoholics is worth reading for what it observes. I have real problems with how it explains what it observes.

Adult Children of Alcoholics
By Janet G. Woititz
Health Communications
1983 (expanded edition 1990)

What the Book Gets Right

Woititz's core observation holds up. Kids who grow up in unstable homes learn to survive them. They learn to anticipate conflict, avoid confrontation, take responsibility for the adults, hide problems, and hold back trust. Those habits made sense in that house. They tend to stop working in adulthood, and a lot of people never notice they are still running them.

Naming those patterns is useful. So is the book's insistence that a child is not responsible for a parent's drinking. That message matters, and Woititz means it.

The 13 Characteristics

The best-known part of the book is her list of 13 characteristics. In her framing, adult children of alcoholics:

  1. Guess at what normal behavior is.
  2. Have difficulty following a project through from beginning to end.
  3. Lie when it would be just as easy to tell the truth.
  4. Judge themselves without mercy.
  5. Have difficulty having fun.
  6. Take themselves very seriously.
  7. Have difficulty with intimate relationships.
  8. Overreact to changes over which they have no control.
  9. Constantly seek approval and affirmation.
  10. Usually feel that they are different from other people.
  11. Are super responsible or super irresponsible.
  12. Are extremely loyal, even when the loyalty is undeserved.
  13. Are impulsive, locking into a course of action without weighing alternatives or consequences.

Most readers will find that some of these fit and some don't. That is the right way to read the list. Woititz herself acknowledges that people from other dysfunctional homes, where the problem was abuse, drug addiction, or something else, can share these traits. So no single trait proves anything about your childhood. Plenty of people take themselves too seriously for reasons that have nothing to do with a bottle.

But a cluster of them, showing up at higher rates, does seem common among people who grew up in that kind of home. That is a fair and useful claim. Observing a pattern is not the same as establishing what caused it.

The Disease Problem

My main disagreement is the book's repeated framing of alcoholism as a disease.

I don't dispute the biology. Addiction changes brain chemistry, reward processing, tolerance, and dependence. Twin, adoption, and alcohol-metabolism research points to inherited vulnerability. Withdrawal from severe dependence can be medically dangerous. All true.

The question is what we call it, and what that label does.

Think about smoking. Smoking is a behavior. It can become an addiction. It can cause a disease, like lung cancer. But smoking is not lung cancer. The behavior, the addiction, and the resulting disease are three different things. The same distinctions apply to alcohol, drugs, gambling, and pornography. Medicine does name tobacco use disorder, substance use disorders, and gambling disorder, so the field is not wildly inconsistent. But whether calling these conditions diseases is scientifically necessary, or partly a philosophical reading of the evidence, is a fair question.

That is my broader concern. Science can establish biological changes, genetic associations, behavior patterns, and treatment outcomes. How we categorize those findings, what language we use, and what moral conclusions follow involves philosophy. That doesn't make the science false or every category arbitrary. It means the interpretation should not be passed off as the data.

And the interpretation has consequences. When disease language puts alcoholism in the same bucket as ALS, Alzheimer's, or cancer, it implies that it simply happened to the person. That hides the agency involved.

Predisposition is not inevitability. Being more susceptible to addiction doesn't mean someone was destined for it. There is also a real difference between the choices that build an addiction and the difficulty of breaking one that is established. Breaking it may take medical treatment, counseling, accountability, community, and a great deal of effort. None of that removes responsibility. Responsibility is actually essential to recovery. The person has to be able to say, "I'm the one who drank. I'm the one who made these decisions. I'm the one who hurt the people around me. I'm responsible for what I did, and for pursuing change."

Alcoholics Anonymous is an interesting case here. I assumed AA might have pushed back on the disease label, given "My name is ___, and I'm an alcoholic." It didn't. AA, founded in 1935, has historically used illness language right alongside moral inventory, confession, making amends, and ongoing accountability. That combination works better than the version of the disease model that turns addiction into an outside force acting on its own. You can hold biological vulnerability and moral agency together. You just have to say both out loud.

"That's the Alcohol Speaking"

One line bothered me more than anything else. The gist was that it's the alcohol speaking, not your dad.

No. That's the dad speaking, because the dad chose to drink to excess.

Alcohol impairs judgment and lowers inhibitions. It is not a second person making decisions on the parent's behalf. The parent is still the one acting. Splitting the alcoholic from his behavior protects the adult and hands the work to the child, who now has to reinterpret his father's cruelty through the lens of an illness instead of being allowed to see it as wrong.

The book is written for adults looking back on that childhood, which makes this worse, not better. They are being taught to retell their own story in a way that softens the person who caused the damage.

What Kids Actually Need to Hear

Here is what I would rather tell a child:

"Your dad made terrible decisions. He is responsible for those decisions and for how they affected you. He needs to own what he did, work to change, and repair the harm where he can. It is right for you to feel hurt, disappointed, or angry about how he behaved when he was supposed to be a loving, responsible father. His choices were not your fault. You didn't cause them, and you are not responsible for fixing him."

Putting the responsibility on the person who did wrong is the right way to tell the person who didn't do wrong that it wasn't their fault. Scripture has been clear on this for a long time: "The one who sins is the one who will die. The child will not share the guilt of the parent, nor will the parent share the guilt of the child" (Ezekiel 18:20, NIV). Guilt stays with the one who earned it, even when the consequences land on others.

Compassion for the alcoholic should not come at the expense of clarity for the child. Disease language probably grew partly out of a good motive: reduce shame so people seek treatment. That is a legitimate goal. But reducing shame should not require reducing accountability.

Should You Read It?

If you grew up in an alcoholic or otherwise chaotic home, the book can help you spot patterns you are still living out. Read the 13 characteristics as a starting point for honest self-examination, not a verdict.

Read the disease framing with your eyes open. The healthier framework is to acknowledge addiction, recognize the biological and psychological factors, provide real treatment and support, and still insist on personal accountability. The adult who drank is responsible for the drinking and the harm. The child is free of responsibility for those choices. And the adult child can acknowledge what that environment did without being permanently defined by it.

Understanding why someone behaved destructively does not excuse the behavior. And knowing how your childhood shaped you does not remove your capacity, or your responsibility, to change.

Three out of five stars. Useful observations, flawed framing.

Reviewed by Scott Asher
